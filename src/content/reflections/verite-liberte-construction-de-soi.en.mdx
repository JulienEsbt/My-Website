## The more I learn, the more wary I become of certainty.

Not because I believe that every idea is equally valid, or that no reality exists beyond our perceptions, but because every human being can only access the world through a singular perspective. That perspective is shaped by our history, culture, experiences, emotions and education, as well as by countless determining forces of which we are often only partly aware.

We observe the same world, but never from the same point of view.

This conviction has gradually drawn me away from philosophical, political and religious systems that claim to possess definitive truths. I identify more closely with certain forms of constructivism, which remind us that our representations of the world are strongly influenced by our social, historical and cultural environment.

Yet I do not believe that individuals are condemned to be nothing more than products of their time, background or determining forces.

Quite the opposite.

I believe we are influenced by many biological, psychological, social and cultural factors. Still, I refuse to see them as fate. Understanding what shapes us does not erase our responsibility; it may even be the first step towards gaining a little more freedom.

## Understanding what determines us

This is where existentialism speaks to me.

The idea that we are not defined once and for all, but build ourselves through our choices, feels profoundly right. We inherit many influences, yet we remain responsible for what we do with them.

Understanding what determines us is not an invitation to abandon responsibility.

I also identify with a moderate form of empiricism. I place great value on lived experience, observation and confrontation with reality. The most elegant ideas interest me very little when they do not withstand the test of the world.

At the same time, I remain cautious whenever anyone claims to possess an ultimate truth.

I believe a reality exists beyond us. But I doubt that an individual, group or ideology can claim to understand it completely. Knowledge progresses, models evolve, and yesterday’s certainties sometimes become tomorrow’s mistakes.

## Doubt as a discipline

Doubt therefore occupies a central place in my thinking.

Not as a weakness or an inability to act, but as an intellectual discipline. To doubt is not to give up thinking. It is to accept that our convictions may be incomplete, imperfect or provisional. It means remaining open to contradiction, reassessment and learning.

This position probably explains my mistrust of labels. Whether philosophical, political or ideological, they often end up confining more than illuminating. I would rather assess ideas through their arguments, consequences and coherence than through the camp with which they are associated.

Likewise, I am more interested in seeking truth than in wanting to be right. In a world saturated with opinions, controversy and displayed certainty, trying to understand seems more useful than trying to prevail.

## I am not trying to build a doctrine

I am trying to understand.

To understand how individuals build themselves.

To understand how societies shape our beliefs.

To understand how we can become freer without denying what determines us.

To understand how to act with greater lucidity, responsibility and consistency.

Perhaps philosophy is ultimately nothing more than this: a permanent attempt to inhabit questions without ceasing to move forward.
